# Commercial AI-Agent Products Built by Solo Developers and Micro-Teams

## Executive finding

The strongest repeatable pattern is **not** “train a better model.” It is to package existing models into a narrow, revenue-linked workflow—building apps, producing videos, acquiring freelance jobs, publishing SEO content, analyzing data, or answering customer questions—then distribute through a channel the founder already controls. Base44, TypingMind, AutoShorts, Subscribr, SEObot, Formula Bot, Lancer, ReplyGuy, SiteGPT, and revid.ai all crossed or plausibly exceeded the requested $5,000 monthly threshold while operating with no more than five people at the relevant revenue snapshot, although the quality of the revenue evidence varies materially.

Five findings are particularly robust:

1. **Speed mattered more than technical novelty.** Base44 reached a $1 million annual run rate roughly three weeks after launch; TypingMind made its first sale on launch day; Subscribr pre-sold approximately $20,000 before building; AutoShorts reached approximately $20,000 MRR within about 90 days; and Lancer had roughly $800 MRR before its public launch. citeturn2search9turn12search7turn5view1turn13search6turn5view0
2. **Distribution was usually founder-led and specific:** an existing X audience, a targeted Reddit community, affiliates with direct access to the buyer, Product Hunt momentum, AppSumo, or paid Facebook ads with measured economics. Generic paid acquisition rarely worked immediately. citeturn12search4turn15search12turn6view0turn16view1turn13search6
3. **The highest-margin architecture externalized model cost.** TypingMind’s customers bring their own API keys, while other products constrained usage through credits, message quotas, or tiered publishing frequencies. citeturn11search3turn14search0turn17search0
4. **Platform dependence was the most common existential risk.** The incidents included a critical authorization flaw, a sudden $5,000 OpenAI bill, social-account bans, an advertising-account block, upstream outages, changing model behavior, and lifetime-deal support obligations. citeturn9search0turn15search12turn16view2turn13search6turn20search0turn12search3
5. **Exact “AI-agent startup” base rates do not exist publicly.** The nearest defensible broad proxy is that roughly **17–20% of newly launched subscription apps reach $1,000 in monthly revenue within two years**, but this is not an AI-agent-specific cohort and should not be presented as one. citeturn19search5turn19search7

## Method and evidence rules

The qualifying revenue window is January 2024 through August 2026. Founding years and time-to-first-dollar may precede 2024 because the request explicitly requires those fields, but the revenue signals used to qualify and rank companies come from the 2024–2026 period wherever possible.

The ranking is a directional composite of:

\[
\text{Rank index} = \text{revenue signal} \times \text{solo scalability} \times \text{time-to-first-dollar}
\]

Revenue was treated logarithmically so an $80 million acquisition would not completely swamp operating businesses. Solo scalability favored one-person operators, then two-person teams, and penalized teams approaching the five-person limit; fast pre-sales and same-week monetization received the highest speed weight, while undisclosed launch timing was penalized.

Revenue labels mean:

- **Public — verified:** a payment-dashboard or Stripe-linked verification.
- **Public — founder-reported:** disclosed by the founder in an interview, post, newsletter, or case study, but not audited.
- **Estimated:** inferred or published by a third-party database; it is not presented as booked revenue.
- **Current team size:** the headcount at the qualifying revenue snapshot, not necessarily the company’s headcount today.

“Tech stack not disclosed” is intentional. An undocumented claim that a product uses LangGraph, CrewAI, AutoGen, or a particular model provider is worse than a missing field.

## Row narratives

**Base44.** Base44 is the cleanest extreme-outlier case: a solo founder converted a broad prompt-to-app product into approximately $3.5 million ARR and an $80 million acquisition in about six months, with the first $1 million ARR run rate reached in roughly three weeks. citeturn2search9turn2search13 Its scalability came from bundling the database, authentication, deployment and model layer rather than selling coding assistance alone, while distribution came from users publishing and sharing working applications instead of screenshots of an assistant. citeturn8search26turn9search19 The qualification is not risk-free: the authorization vulnerability demonstrates that an agent capable of generating production systems can propagate platform-level security mistakes across every generated customer application. citeturn9search0turn9search11

## Base rate and scope audit

### The defensible base rate

There is **no public dataset that tracks all AI-agent startups launched in 2023–2024 and reports what percentage reached $1,000 MRR by 2026**. Startup databases omit failed and quietly abandoned projects, while payment processors usually classify by application category rather than whether software is an “agent,” “assistant,” “workflow,” or ordinary AI feature.

The nearest broad subscription-software proxies are:

| Proxy | Result | Limitation |
|---|---:|---|
| RevenueCat 2024 cohort, almost 30,000 subscription apps | **17.2%** crossed $1,000 in monthly revenue; only **3.5%** reached $10,000 monthly. citeturn19search5 | Predominantly mobile subscription apps; not specifically AI agents or bootstrapped SaaS. |
| RevenueCat 2025 newly launched app cohort | **Nearly 20%** reached $1,000 monthly revenue within two years; roughly **5%** reached $10,000. citeturn19search7 | Again, all subscription categories rather than agents. |
| RevenueCat 2026 AI-app retention | AI apps converted trials to paid at **8.5% versus 5.6%** for non-AI apps, but annual subscriber retention was only **21.1% versus 30.7%**. citeturn19search0 | Measures subscriber behavior among apps that launched and instrumented subscriptions, not startup-level survival. |

Therefore, the honest answer is:

> **Agent-specific percentage: unknown. Nearest broad prior: approximately 17–20% reach $1K monthly revenue within two years, and only about 4–5% reach $10K monthly.**

It would be inappropriate to claim that 17–20% of AI-agent startups succeed. AI apps show stronger early monetization but materially worse long-term retention, and the agent category contains a large unobserved population of GitHub projects, demos and products that never enabled billing. citeturn19search0turn19search7

### Survivorship bias

The ten exemplars are not representative. They were discoverable because founders published revenue, appeared in case studies, connected Stripe data to a directory, or completed a visible acquisition; companies that quietly remained at $0–$900 MRR rarely produce comparable records.

A useful operating prior is consequently harsher than the table makes it look:

- Hitting $5,000 MRR places a product well above the median subscription launch.
- Reaching it does not imply durability; AI apps’ weak annual retention means a founder can cross the threshold and fall below it rapidly. citeturn19search0
- Acquisition-channel concentration and inference costs can destroy a fast-growing product before annual revenue catches up with headline MRR.

### Coverage of the requested platform scope

No direct qualifying exemplar with sufficiently strong evidence was found among Cursor, Devin, Replit Agent, Cline, Aider, OpenHands, Continue, Windsurf/Codeium, Cody or GitHub Copilot Workspace. The commercial platform companies generally exceeded five people, while the open-source projects generally did not disclose a separately attributable $5,000-plus monthly commercial product run by five or fewer people.

Likewise, no CrewAI, AutoGen, LangGraph, DSPy or Portkey implementation met **all four** filters simultaneously: a named commercial product, no more than five people, at least $5,000 monthly revenue, and public evidence of both revenue and architecture. The absence from the ranking is an evidence decision, not a claim that no such businesses exist.

Browser/computer-use products presented the same issue. MultiOn, Adept and Manus were not micro-teams by the relevant commercial period, while browser-use’s ecosystem had numerous consulting and integration businesses but insufficient public founder-level revenue records to rank one without speculation.
