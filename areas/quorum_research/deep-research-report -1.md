# Solo and Tiny-Team Spatial-Computing Revenue, 2024–2026

## Executive verdict

There **are** credible 2024–2026 examples of solo developers and teams of five or fewer earning money from mixed reality, VR, spatial utilities, unusual motion input, and hand tracking. The strongest verified cases are concentrated on **Meta Quest**, followed by paid PC-VR releases on Steam. Apple Vision Pro has produced some genuine solo-developer revenue, but the most transparent example found—Adam Roszyk’s portfolio of roughly 30 monetized visionOS apps—had generated only about **$10,000 in cumulative gross sales** by July 2026, with one app accounting for approximately 41% of the portfolio. citeturn3search0turn8search28turn3search11

The highest-upside qualifying case is **Animal Company**, launched by a five-person founding team in July 2024. Meta reports that it became the fifth-highest-grossing Quest title by first-year revenue, reached roughly one million monthly active users and 500,000 daily active users, and grew paying users ninefold within six months after delaying monetization until retention was proven. Exact revenue remains undisclosed, but multi-million-dollar gross revenue is a reasonable inference rather than a reported number. citeturn6search0turn6search1turn6search9turn7search4

The practical picture is highly uneven:

| Finding | Evidence-based conclusion |
|---|---|
| Best current platform for tiny teams | Meta Quest, especially premium games with a sharp physical mechanic or free-to-play social games with strong retention. Meta says Quest developer payments increased about 12% in 2024 and that more than 100 Quest apps generated over $1 million gross during 2025. citeturn20search3turn20search14 |
| Best truly solo business pattern | A small, easily demonstrated paid experience or utility, not a broad “spatial platform.” Laser Dance, Scan Export, Toy Monsters and Vendetta Forever fit this pattern. |
| Most dangerous pattern | A content-heavy VR campaign built by one or two people before validating polish, comfort and demand. HEARTSHOT and COLD VR illustrate the downside. citeturn14search2turn18search2turn18search4 |
| Apple Vision Pro viability | Real but generally supplemental rather than salary-replacing for transparent solo developers. Apple reported more than 2,500 native spatial apps by August 2024, while the clearest public solo portfolio found generated only $10,000 gross across about 30 monetized apps. citeturn20search7turn3search0 |
| Webcam-gesture living-wage case | **No publicly verifiable case found.** This means no case meeting the requested evidence standard, not proof that no private or undisclosed business exists. |

The best defensible base-rate estimate is that approximately **5% of commercially released indie spatial or AR apps ever touch $1,000 in a single month**, with a plausible range of **3%–8%**. Sustaining $1,000 per month for a year is probably closer to **2%–5%**. These are modeled estimates, not measured industry statistics; no platform publishes the required indie-only revenue distribution.

## Scope and evidence standard

The research window was restricted to products commercially launched, substantially relaunched, or first monetized between **January 1, 2024 and August 1, 2026**. Older success stories were excluded even when their developers posted new financial updates during the window. For example, GymStreak appeared in a December 2024 Starter Story article reporting approximately $208,000 in revenue and AR features, but the underlying business predates 2024 and therefore was not used as a ranked exemplar. citeturn4search1

Team size refers to the **core development team at launch**, not necessarily the company’s later headcount. Publisher personnel, marketing contractors and platform support were separated from core development where public credits allowed it. This distinction matters: Animal Company qualified at founding with five people but subsequently expanded, while several apparently “indie” VR successes were produced by studios much larger than five people. citeturn6search0turn6search2

Revenue evidence was classified as follows:

- **Disclosed:** a developer, publisher or platform explicitly stated a monetary amount.
- **Rank or operational evidence:** a platform disclosed gross-ranking, user, retention or payer data but no dollar figure.
- **Modeled:** revenue was approximated from public prices, ratings or third-party ownership estimates.

For Quest estimates, the report uses a public heuristic derived from analyst Cassia Curran’s work: approximately **110 purchases per store rating at the median**. This is only an order-of-magnitude indicator. Free trials, refunds, discounts, regional pricing, review propensity and store changes can move an individual title far away from the median. All rating-derived figures below should therefore be read with error bars of at least **two- to threefold**. citeturn5search0turn6search18

“Gross” means customer spending before Meta, Valve, Apple or a publisher takes its share, and before taxes, refunds, contractor costs and development expenses. A $250,000 modeled gross does not mean the developer personally received $250,000.

### Laser Dance

Thomas Van Bouwel, previously known for Cubism, built Laser Dance primarily as a solo developer. Public credits additionally identify business-development and programming support, keeping the visible team within the requested five-person ceiling. The $9.99 Quest release had 257 ratings at the research cutoff, producing a rough modeled gross of $282,000 under the 110-purchases-per-rating heuristic. citeturn15search8turn16search0turn10search27

What worked was unusually clear product communication: a Quest user immediately understands “turn my room into a laser obstacle course.” It is physical, spectator-friendly, recordable in short video, and useful as a party demonstration. Awards and public demonstrations generated credibility before full commercial scale. citeturn15search8turn10search27

The weakness is environmental variability. A room-scale game must handle different room dimensions, furniture, scene meshes and user mobility. That QA burden is disproportionate for one person. Nevertheless, this is probably the most relevant template for a developer who already has a compelling spatial demo: one mechanic, one room, low content requirements and a sub-$15 impulse price.

### Adam Roszyk’s visionOS portfolio and Scan Export

Adam Roszyk publicly reported crossing **$10,000 in cumulative customer sales** from a solo visionOS portfolio by July 24, 2026. Roughly 30 apps had earned at least some money, and Scan Export accounted for approximately 41% of the total. Scan Export uses Vision Pro’s spatial sensors to scan rooms and export models such as USDZ or STL files. citeturn3search0turn8search28turn3search11turn3search12

What worked was portfolio diversification and utility-first development. Instead of wagering years on one cinematic experience, Roszyk repeatedly shipped small apps around capabilities that were new or awkward on the platform. The concentration of 41% in one product also shows why multiple shots were useful: the developer could not have predicted exactly which capability would carry the portfolio.

What failed, in business terms, is scale. Ten thousand dollars spread across roughly 30 monetized applications and more than a year of platform availability is proof of customer demand, but not evidence of a sustainable salary. This is the clearest warning against interpreting “first mover on Vision Pro” as automatic commercial advantage.

### Vendetta Forever

Vendetta Forever was made by solo developer Zander Dejah under Meatspace Interactive and published by nDreams. The game launched on Quest and PS VR2 in October 2024 and reached Steam in May 2025. Its central “Lo-Kill-Motion” system teleports the player to weapons dropped by killed enemies, combining locomotion and combat into one visually legible mechanic. citeturn12search5turn12search25turn12search0

At the cutoff, the Quest listing showed 209 ratings at $19.99. The rating heuristic implies approximately $460,000 in Quest consumer spending, before accounting for discounts and error. Steam had only 34 purchaser reviews, meaning PC VR was probably a much smaller contributor; PS VR2 revenue is not public. citeturn12search1turn12search0

What worked was a mechanic that could be explained and recognized in seconds, high critical reception, and a publisher capable of securing multi-platform placement. What did not work as strongly was organic scale: despite strong reviews and awards, the public rating counts remain modest compared with breakout Quest titles. The product is solo-replicable; the publisher access, console certification and launch visibility are not.

### Animal Company

Animal Company began with a five-person team and launched in early access in July 2024. Rather than immediately optimizing purchases, Wooster Games spent the first months validating engagement. Monetization was introduced approximately three months later after the game showed around 100 minutes of average daily play and 45% 28-day retention. Paying users subsequently grew ninefold over six months. Meta also reported roughly one million monthly active users, 500,000 daily active users and a fifth-place ranking among first-year Quest titles by gross revenue. citeturn6search0turn6search1turn6search9turn7search4

What worked was not “spatial computing” as a novelty. It was a social behavior loop: chaotic multiplayer interactions, youth-oriented identity, user-generated clips and continuous live content. The game reportedly accumulated approximately one billion TikTok views without relying on conventional paid user acquisition. citeturn6search12turn7search4

This is replicable only in layers. Five people can prototype the movement, social chaos and clip-worthy interactions. A solo developer cannot realistically operate the moderation, economy, servers, weekly content, community and support functions of a successful youth-focused live service. The correct lesson is to copy the **retention-first sequencing**, not the product’s eventual operational scope.

### Toy Monsters

Toy Monsters was built by solo developer Habaduda Games and published by Beyond Frames. It brought a familiar lane-defense structure into the user’s room, supporting passthrough mixed reality and hand tracking. It moved from App Lab to the main Quest store in April 2024 at $14.99. citeturn15search12turn17search5turn17search1

At approximately 139 Quest ratings, the standard heuristic suggests around $229,000 in gross customer spending. Beyond Frames reported that sales were meeting expectations and that it expected a positive return on the publishing investment, although it did not disclose title-level revenue. citeturn16search2turn17search26

The successful design decision was familiarity: users did not need to learn an entirely new genre and a new input method simultaneously. The spatial layer changed where the lanes and enemies appeared, while the underlying tower-defense logic remained understandable. The limitation is that the commercially finished product depended on a publisher, and the rating count suggests respectable niche performance rather than a breakout.

### MiRacle Pool

MiRacle Pool launched on the primary Quest store in June 2024 after an App Lab period. Pixel Works Studios described itself only as a small indie VR/MR studio, so its precise compliance with the five-person ceiling cannot be independently confirmed. The team said it spent more than three years refining its billiards physics. citeturn11search7turn11search11turn11search22

The current listing’s roughly 1,400 ratings and $19.99 price imply about $3.1 million gross under the median Quest conversion heuristic. Because the initial price was about $15 and the game has been discounted, a more cautious range is **$2 million–$3.1 million**. This estimate is especially uncertain and should not be treated as disclosed revenue. citeturn9search18turn11search7turn5search0

What worked was depth in one physical simulation rather than breadth: recognizable rules, multiplayer, daily challenges, progression and a persistent reason to return. What likely raised the development burden was the requirement that ball physics and cue handling feel credible. A rough pool prototype is solo-replicable; the three-year refinement and ongoing multiplayer support are less so.

### HEARTSHOT

HEARTSHOT was built by a two-person Meadow Studios team and entered Steam early access in March 2024 at $14.99. It combined a campaign shooter with magnet gloves for retrieving weapons and manipulating the environment. citeturn14search0turn14search2

A third-party Steam estimate lists approximately 1,687 owners, corresponding to roughly $25,000 at full list price before discounts, refunds and Valve’s share. Its 265 Steam reviews were only about 60% positive at the research cutoff. citeturn14search1turn14search2turn14search10

The positive lesson is that two developers can ship a visually substantial VR shooter. The negative lesson is more important: narrative campaigns multiply the work in level design, art, enemy behavior, animation, voice, pacing and QA, while every hour of content is consumed once. The mixed review score and extended early-access structure indicate that the team shipped before achieving consistently acceptable polish.

### COLD VR

COLD VR was the solo passion project of Carlos Alfonso, operating as ALLWARE, and was published by Perp Games. It launched on Quest and Steam in February 2025 at $19.99. Its hook reversed SUPERHOT’s time mechanic: motion slows time, while stopping allows time to accelerate. citeturn13search9turn13search12turn18search1

Games-Stats estimates approximately $10,000 in Steam gross revenue. The Quest listing had 29 ratings, which implies approximately $64,000 in gross Quest spending under the median-rating heuristic. These figures suggest perhaps $70,000–$80,000 combined before PS VR2, but the uncertainty is very high. citeturn18search0turn18search2turn5search0

What worked was a pitch that could be communicated in one sentence and a publishing relationship that enabled multiple platforms. What failed was execution quality. UploadVR found frustrating level design, inconsistent interactions, visual downgrades on Quest and an overall lack of polish; Steam reviews were also mixed. citeturn18search1turn18search4

This is a useful counterexample to “novel input equals revenue.” The concept attracted attention, but novelty did not compensate for friction in level design and interaction reliability.

### Jigsaw Night

Jigsaw Night was built by solo developer Steve Lukas as a colocated and remote social jigsaw-puzzle experience for Quest. It uses hand tracking, shared spatial anchors and interactions designed to let multiple players work around the same virtual table. Reviewers highlighted its interaction design, and UploadVR selected it among the leading hand-tracking and mixed-reality experiences of 2025. citeturn17search0turn17search4turn15search9

The title is commercially available, but no developer revenue disclosure or sufficiently reliable public sales estimate was found. It is therefore included as a **commercially relevant but financially unquantified** case, not as proof of a living wage.

What worked was a narrow activity already understood by mainstream users, combined with unusually careful interaction design. The principal business risk is hardware coordination: the strongest experience involves multiple headsets, reducing the number of households that can immediately use its differentiating feature. citeturn17search4turn17search24

### UG

UG is the strongest revenue benchmark that did not fully pass the team-size verification test. Meta stated that UG earned **millions of dollars** in 2025. Public reporting described average player engagement above 14 hours, roughly one hour of daily play, average revenue per user above $14 and extended placement at or near the top of Quest’s sales rankings. The Quest listing had hundreds of thousands of ratings. citeturn7search2turn7search12turn7search17

Continuum has been described as using a small development and art team, but no authoritative public source found in this research established that the launch team contained five or fewer people. It should therefore not be counted as a verified ≤5-person success.

The business lesson reinforces Animal Company: major Quest revenue increasingly comes from social retention, cosmetics, repeated sessions and creator-driven visibility. Those systems are much harder to operate than the initial prototype.

## What the cases actually teach

### A demonstrable physical verb beats abstract spatial ambition

The strongest tiny-team products can be understood through a single verb:

- dodge lasers;
- scan a room;
- shoot and teleport;
- arrange towers on a table;
- play pool in your room;
- solve a puzzle with visible hands.

These concepts compress well into store trailers and short social clips. By contrast, generic claims such as “a new spatial platform,” “an immersive metaverse” or “gesture-controlled experiences” do not explain why a consumer should return or pay.

Laser Dance, Vendetta Forever and Toy Monsters all preserved a conventional game objective while using spatial input to make that objective feel new. citeturn16search0turn12search1turn17search5

### The viable solo scope is smaller than the technical demo suggests

A developer may be able to build a hand-tracking or scene-understanding demo in days. Commercialization adds failure modes that the demo does not expose:

- room and lighting variation;
- occlusion and tracking loss;
- accessibility and seated use;
- onboarding users unfamiliar with spatial interfaces;
- device performance and thermals;
- refunds caused by discomfort;
- store positioning and trailer production;
- continued support after headset firmware changes.

COLD VR and HEARTSHOT show that a technically functional, substantial VR game can still struggle because the aggregate interaction and content polish is insufficient. citeturn18search4turn14search2

### Publishers still matter for premium VR

Vendetta Forever, Toy Monsters and COLD VR were all solo-developed at the core but had publishers. Publishers assisted with cross-platform distribution, store relationships, marketing, certification or production support. Their existence means the products are valid solo-development cases but not examples of a single person independently performing every commercial function. citeturn12search25turn17search31turn13search9

The replicable strategy is therefore not necessarily “self-publish everything.” It is to reach a polished, easily demonstrated vertical slice and then use the slice to secure distribution assistance.

### Utilities can monetize earlier but have lower ceilings

Scan Export required less content than a game and addressed a specific workflow made possible by Vision Pro’s hardware. That allowed a solo developer to ship repeatedly and earn real revenue. The disclosed portfolio results nevertheless show a low ceiling in the current Vision Pro installed base. citeturn3search0turn3search11

For a solo developer, a $5–$30 spatial utility may have a better risk-adjusted return than a two-year game. It is not currently strong evidence for replacing a senior developer salary.

### Free-to-play only wins after retention exists

Animal Company did not add monetization immediately. The developers first measured unusually strong daily play and 28-day retention, then introduced purchases. That order matters: adding skins to an experience with poor retention does not create a business. citeturn6search1turn7search4

A solo developer should not interpret Animal Company as encouragement to build a large live-service economy. The practical lesson is to instrument a free or low-cost prototype, establish repeated voluntary use, and only then decide whether live operations are justified.

## Base rate

No authoritative source publishes the requested statistic: the percentage of indie spatial or AR apps that ever reach $1,000 per month. The denominator is especially problematic because store catalogs contain free experiments, abandoned App Lab projects, enterprise utilities, conventional VR games, compatibility-mode apps and products from large studios.

The most useful public anchor is Quest. Around the 2024 App Lab merger, public estimates placed the Quest catalog at roughly **2,860–3,000 applications**. Meta subsequently stated that more than **100 Quest apps generated over $1 million gross in 2025 alone**. A crude division gives approximately 3.3%, although the dates and catalog definitions do not perfectly align. citeturn20search7turn20search22turn20search14

That 3.3% is notable because $1 million per year is vastly harder than $1,000 per month. It establishes that the Quest share reaching $1,000 in at least one month must be higher than roughly 3%, but it does not reveal how much higher. It also includes established studios and older titles rather than only post-2024 solo releases.

Vision Pro provides the opposite signal. Apple reported more than 600 native spatial apps at launch and more than 2,500 by August 2024. Yet the most transparent solo portfolio found generated only $10,000 gross across approximately 30 monetized apps by July 2026. This does not measure the whole distribution, but it suggests that a large portion of native visionOS software remains well below $1,000 per month. citeturn20search4turn20search7turn3search0

The resulting working model is:

| Threshold | Best estimate | Plausible range | Confidence |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Ever reaches $1,000 in one calendar month | **5%** | **3%–8%** | Low |
| Sustains at least $1,000/month for twelve months | **3%** | **2%–5%** | Very low |
| Replaces a US professional developer salary | **Below 1%** | **0.3%–1%** | Very low |
| Quest premium/social apps with a polished release | Higher than overall base rate | Possibly 8%–15% reaching one $1,000 month | Low |
| Native Vision Pro consumer apps | Lower than overall base rate | Probably below 3% sustaining $1,000/month | Very low |
| Webcam-gesture consumer apps | No measurable base rate | Verified public successes: zero found | Very low |

The honest answer is therefore not “X% has been measured.” It is: **approximately one in twenty reaches a $1,000 month, but the public evidence is too incomplete to distinguish one in ten from one in thirty with confidence.**

## Webcam-gesture verdict

**No—no publicly verifiable 2024–2026 case was found of a solo developer living primarily from a consumer application whose central input is hand or body gestures captured through an ordinary webcam.**

This conclusion is deliberately narrower than saying such a business cannot exist. It means the targeted source search did not find a case with all of the following:

1. a named solo developer;
2. a consumer rather than enterprise product;
3. ordinary webcam input as the core interaction rather than an optional feature;
4. a 2024–2026 commercial launch or revenue period;
5. disclosed or independently estimable revenue sufficient to support a full-time living.

The closest results failed one or more tests. Puppetstring is a webcam face-tracking tool associated with the Inochi2D ecosystem, but its developer disclosed product development rather than revenue sufficient for a living. The itch.io hand-tracking category contained only 39 VR projects at the cutoff, many of them free, experimental or old, and no qualifying developer-income disclosure surfaced. citeturn21search10turn21search0

Other browser webcam-hand-tracking results were tutorials, demos, portfolio projects, research prototypes or generic gesture-control extensions rather than documented consumer businesses. citeturn19search8turn19search19turn19search24

Web-game platforms do prove that solo and small-team browser developers can earn substantial revenue. Poki advertised more than €100,000 in 2024 earnings per active developer, and one developer reported 67 million Poki plays across five conventional games during 2025. Poki also says some browser-game developers now earn as much as $1 million annually. None of the identified cases, however, used WebXR or webcam gestures as the primary consumer input. citeturn21search9turn21search7turn21search17

The commercial problem is not only tracking accuracy. Webcam gestures add permission friction, privacy concerns, lighting and framing failures, arm fatigue and poor laptop ergonomics while competing against mouse, keyboard and touchscreen input that already works reliably. The evidence indicates that webcam gesture is currently better treated as a **viral feature, accessibility option, installation, marketing demonstration or B2B capability** than as the sole foundation of a mass-market consumer business.

## Source audit and limitations

The evidence was strongest in official Meta developer material, Quest store pages, Steam store data, developer disclosures and specialist VR publications.

**Indie Hackers, Starter Story and build-in-public posts:** targeted searches did not produce a qualifying post-2024 spatial or webcam-gesture revenue case. The closest revenue-bearing Starter Story result involved an older fitness business and was excluded. Indie Hackers results with disclosed 2025–2026 revenue were conventional mobile or SaaS products rather than spatial applications. citeturn4search1turn19search13

**Apple App Store and mobile intelligence:** public Sensor Tower/Video Game Insights pages supplied useful Steam-level estimates for products such as Vendetta Forever, COLD VR and HEARTSHOT, but a complete, auditable public table of 2024–2026 App Store or Google Play top-grossing spatial/camera apps with developer headcounts was not available. Consequently, no paywalled mobile estimate was presented as fact. citeturn12search2turn13search2turn14search3

**Steam:** store reviews and third-party ownership estimates identified real revenue, but the tiny PC-VR audience and large variance in purchases per review make these estimates approximate. HEARTSHOT and COLD VR demonstrate that shipping on Steam does not imply sustainable income. citeturn14search1turn18search2

**Meta Quest:** this was the only channel with both substantial ecosystem-level revenue disclosures and multiple tiny-team examples. Meta reports more than $2 billion spent on Quest content by March 2025, developer payments up roughly 12% during 2024 and more than 100 applications exceeding $1 million gross during 2025. citeturn20search3turn20search14

**Product Hunt:** no qualifying 2024–2026 AR, spatial, gesture or webcam launch with a credible tracked revenue disclosure surfaced. Upvotes and launch rankings were not treated as revenue.

**itch.io:** the platform contains paid hand-tracking experiments, but the category was small and no qualifying 2024–2026 income disclosure was found. A listed price proves a monetization mechanism, not meaningful sales. citeturn21search0

**Poki and CrazyGames:** meaningful browser-game income clearly exists, but the public success cases found were standard HTML5/WebGL games, not WebXR or webcam-input applications. Platform-wide developer averages cannot be transferred to an input niche with no identified hit. citeturn21search9turn21search13turn21search15

**Team-size uncertainty:** MiRacle Pool and UG remain conditional rather than fully verified ≤5-person cases. Public descriptions such as “small indie studio” are insufficient to assert a precise headcount.

**Revenue-estimate uncertainty:** Quest rating models are directional. They should be used to separate likely five-figure products from likely six- or seven-figure products, not to claim exact sales.
